How it actually works

No magic, no hidden server. Here's exactly what happens to your pixels.

You mark the watermark

Drag a rectangle over the logo, timestamp, or text. Regions are stored as fractions of the frame, so they scale correctly whatever the resolution.

We reconstruct the hole from its edges

The marked pixels become a hole in the image. A multiscale diffusion solver fills it by propagating colour inward from the surrounding border — the same family of technique as Poisson inpainting in Photoshop's healing brush, just written in plain JavaScript.

The patch is blended back

The filled area is composited with a smooth falloff at the edges, so there's no visible seam where the repair meets the original footage.

Frames are re-encoded locally

Every processed frame is drawn to a canvas and captured by the browser's own encoder. Nothing is transmitted. When it's done, you get a file back.

Why this one is different

There is no server

The whole app is a single HTML file. Once it's loaded you can disconnect from the internet and it still works. Nothing to log, nothing to leak.

No artificial limits

No ten-second caps, no 720p ceiling, no daily quota, no watermark added to the output to make you upgrade. Your hardware is the only limit.

Readable source

MIT licensed and about a thousand lines. Audit the inpainting code yourself, fork it, or self-host it on your own domain in a couple of minutes.

Questions

Is it really free, or is there a catch?
Really free. There's no account system, no payment processor, and no metered API — the computation happens on your CPU, so there's nothing for us to bill you for. The project is MIT licensed and hosted on static hosting that costs nothing to run.
Where does my video go?
Nowhere. The file is read into browser memory via a local blob URL and never leaves the page. You can verify this by opening DevTools → Network and watching that no requests fire while you process a clip. Better still: load the page, then turn off your Wi-Fi.
How good is the result, honestly?
It's very good on smooth backgrounds — sky, walls, gradients, shallow depth-of-field, static grain, solid colour. On busy or highly detailed backgrounds you'll see some softening or smearing in the repaired area, because the algorithm is reconstructing detail that isn't there. If a competitor claims perfect removal on complex footage, they're either using a generative model or lying.
Can it remove a moving watermark?
Not with a single box. If the watermark drifts across the frame you'd need to keyframe it, which this tool doesn't do yet. If it's in the same place for the whole clip — which is the case for most broadcast logos, timestamps, and stock-footage marks — you're fine.
Why does exporting take as long as the video itself?
Browser video encoding through MediaRecorder runs against the wall clock, so the clip plays through once while frames are captured. A two-minute video takes about two minutes. Faster-than-realtime export is possible with the WebCodecs API and is on the roadmap.
What format do I get back?
Whichever your browser can encode best — usually MP4 (H.264) in Chrome and Safari, WebM (VP9) in Firefox. Audio is preserved where the browser allows it. Output bitrate is scaled to resolution automatically.
Is this legal to use?
Depends entirely on what you're removing and why. Stripping a watermark from content you don't own or have a licence for is copyright infringement in most jurisdictions, and removing attribution can breach platform terms. Common legitimate uses: cleaning up footage you shot yourself, removing a stock preview mark after licensing, clearing a client's logo from a rough cut, or de-cluttering archive material you hold rights to. That's on you to get right.
